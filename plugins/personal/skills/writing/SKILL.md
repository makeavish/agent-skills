# Writing

Help the writer say what they mean with less reader effort and more reader interest. Treat the guidance below as a set of craft tools, not a house style. Preserve the writer's facts, intent, level of certainty, and recognizable voice.

## Choose the mode

Identify what the user actually wants before changing the prose:

- **Draft** — turn notes, an outline, or an idea into finished prose.
- **Rewrite** — improve supplied prose while preserving its meaning and voice.
- **Critique** — diagnose the highest-impact problems without silently replacing the draft.
- **Unblock** — help the writer generate raw material, discover the real problem, or find a viable direction.

When the request is clear, begin. Ask a question only when a missing answer would materially change the result. Otherwise infer a reasonable audience, purpose, format, and tone from context and mention an important assumption briefly.

## Protect the writer's intent

Before editing, determine:

- Who will read this?
- What should they understand, feel, or do afterward?
- What form and length does the situation require?
- What voice already belongs to the writer?
- Is this nonfiction, fiction, or a hybrid?

Keep claims, numbers, quotations, citations, names, links, Markdown, and technical meaning intact unless the user asks to change them. Do not invent evidence, anecdotes, credentials, sources, or lived experience to make a piece livelier. Use an explicit placeholder when essential material is missing.

When drafting new prose, let the facts carry their own weight. Avoid stock importance claims such as `marks a pivotal moment` unless the material establishes why, and name the source behind claims such as `experts agree` or leave them out.

Preserve factual meaning, not weak scaffolding. A request to retain the facts does not require keeping empty frames such as `is something that`, `is a thing that`, or `is important to consider`; state the underlying claim directly.

Distinguish a polish from a creative expansion. In a polish or close rewrite, first recombine details already on the page; do not silently add gestures, props, sensations, elapsed time, or backstory. Add new scene material when the user requests a fresh draft or creative rewrite, or present it as an optional suggestion.

Do not confuse clarity with blandness. Retain deliberate fragments, unusual diction, repetition, humor, technical precision, and genre conventions when they serve the piece.

## Work in passes

Separate creation from judgment. Trying to solve ideas, structure, sentences, and polish at once often causes writer's block and shallow edits.

1. **Find the material.** For a new draft, generate rough claims, images, examples, questions, or scene possibilities without polishing each sentence. For writer's block, produce an intentionally imperfect continuation or several possible directions; use the bad draft to reveal what is wrong.
2. **Build the spine.** State the piece's central promise or thesis in one sentence. Group related material. Give every section a job and every paragraph one main move.
3. **Draft for movement.** Lead the reader from familiar context to new information, and from concrete material toward interpretation. Signal how each part advances the whole.
4. **Revise for clarity.** Fix missing context, weak logic, vague abstraction, ambiguity, and avoidable processing effort.
5. **Revise for rhythm and interest.** Vary sentence movement, strengthen useful surprises and payoffs, and remove stretches that merely repeat or stall.
6. **Proofread last.** Check grammar, punctuation, spelling, formatting, and consistency only after the larger decisions are stable.

For a short rewrite, compress these passes mentally rather than narrating them to the user.

## Shape the structure

- Open with a substantive observation, concrete situation, consequential claim, or honest unanswered question. Avoid throat-clearing, dictionary openings, and claims about what people have believed "throughout history."
- If the opening raises a question or promises a result, pay it off. Curiosity without delivery feels like clickbait.
- Give each paragraph an explicit or implied mini-thesis. If a paragraph makes two unrelated moves, split it. If it makes none, strengthen or remove it.
- Test a long argument by listing its paragraph mini-theses in order. They should form a coherent compressed version of the piece.
- Put related ideas near one another. Introduce context before the detail that depends on it; move from given information to new information.
- Start a difficult explanation with a concrete case. Use more than one example when readers need to triangulate the underlying idea.
- Name recurring complex ideas with memorable, accurate concept handles, then use those handles consistently.
- Make relationships explicit: evidence for a claim, objection and response, cause and effect, step and consequence.
- Keep paragraphs compact enough to scan, but do not enforce a sentence limit when an argument or emotional beat needs room.
- End sections on an insight, consequence, decision, image, or question that creates useful forward motion.

## Edit the sentences

### Increase information value

- Prefer specific nouns and strong verbs when they add real information.
- Remove filters and qualifiers that contribute neither meaning nor voice.
- Turn dummy constructions into direct subject-verb claims: `X is something that helps Y` becomes `X helps Y`.
- Cut duplicate meaning, not necessary context, atmosphere, or emotional processing time.
- Move down the ladder of abstraction when the reader needs to picture or test a claim.

Examples:

- `I could see the neighbor's greyhound moving across the road.` -> `The neighbor's greyhound loped across the road.`
- `The rollout was somewhat delayed in nature.` -> `The rollout was delayed.`
- `She checked the clock twice and wiped her palm on her jeans. She felt anxious.` -> `She checked the clock twice and wiped her palm on her jeans.`

### Control attention

Choose active or passive voice according to what belongs in the reader's mental spotlight. Active voice usually makes an event easier to picture because it supplies an actor. Passive voice is useful when the receiver, result, or unknown actor is the point.

### Create rhythm

- Vary sentence length and sentence openings so the prose does not drone.
- Use short sentences for emphasis and longer ones when the reader can follow the build.
- Preserve parallel structure when it clarifies a comparison or creates deliberate momentum.
- Treat repeated words or syntax as conspicuous. Keep repetition when it emphasizes the point; revise it when it is accidental.
- Read the passage aloud, or simulate doing so, to catch breathless clauses, awkward cadence, and hidden ambiguity.

### Remove ambiguity

Check pronouns, modifiers, attachment, scope, chronology, and transitions at sentence and sub-sentence level. Rewrite any sentence that invites a plausible wrong reading before the intended reading becomes clear.

Prefer `because` when causal `since` could initially be read as temporal. Replace stacked nouns or unexplained abbreviations when they force the reader to decode rather than understand.

### Make technical prose hospitable

Briefly explain an unfamiliar term at first use without talking down to the reader. Follow abstractions with `for example`, `such as`, or an equivalent concrete case. Preserve exact technical distinctions; simplicity that changes the claim is not clarity.

## Hold the reader's attention

Reward attention with some combination of useful insight, concrete detail, surprise, progress, emotional movement, or restrained humor. Do not force all of them into every piece.

- Break a long piece at genuine conceptual turns so readers can register progress.
- Shorten or strengthen stretches that offer no new insight, example, consequence, or pleasure.
- Use microhumor only when it fits the writer and context. A legal warning, incident report, or apology usually needs restraint.
- Keep hooks honest and specific. Do not exaggerate stakes the body cannot support.
- Put the sharpest word late in a sentence when doing so improves emphasis without distorting natural syntax.

## Nonfiction pass

For essays, articles, explanations, documentation, memos, and persuasive writing:

1. State the main claim or purpose plainly enough that the structure can be tested against it.
2. Check every paragraph's mini-thesis and the logic between them.
3. Orient the reader at a useful level, descend into examples or evidence, then return to a stronger synthesis.
4. Explain specialized terms at the reader's altitude and supply concrete examples.
5. Surface the strongest likely objection fairly. Answer it when the piece is argumentative; do not hide a genuine limitation.
6. Verify that the conclusion earns its confidence and resolves the opening promise.

Do not apply fiction advice such as sensory detail or "show, don't tell" mechanically to operational documentation. In instructional prose, directness is often the better craft choice.

## Fiction pass

For stories, scenes, and dialogue, apply only the tools relevant to the user's genre and intent:

- Establish what important characters want, what blocks them, and what they may need to change. Connect quirks and choices to motivation rather than attaching random traits.
- Let characters make intelligent attempts to improve their situation. Do not make them foolish merely to protect the plot; shape circumstances so believable choices still create consequences.
- Build conflict through competing values, goals, and status—not only surface disagreement. A web of pressures often creates more depth than one simple opponent.
- Track the story's promises and deliver at least the payoff the reader was led to expect.
- For an emotional beat, identify the obvious emotion, then ask twice, "What else is this character feeling?" Explore the less expected layer through action, speech, image, and what this character alone notices.
- Give speakers distinct diction, rhythms, metaphors, evasions, and argumentative habits. Dialogue should reveal motivation, not merely transfer plot information.
- Keep author, narrator, and viewpoint character conceptually distinct. Vary psychic distance deliberately so the reader can both inhabit and observe the character.
- Introduce only the worldbuilding the scene needs. Deepen existing elements before adding new ones, and consider how a fantastic premise changes ordinary behavior and institutions.
- Treat theme as tension between ideals the story can genuinely respect, not a contest between a full person and a caricature.

## Deliver the result

Match the response to the requested mode:

- **Draft or rewrite:** give the finished prose first. Add a short `What changed` note only when the changes are substantial, the user requested rationale, or a factual/structural choice needs confirmation.
- **Critique:** lead with the highest-impact diagnosis, then give prioritized, specific fixes. Quote only the smallest fragment needed to locate an issue. Verify every count and quotation against the supplied text; a critique must be at least as factually careful as the prose it examines. Do not replace the whole piece unless asked.
- **Unblock:** offer raw material the writer can react to—a deliberately rough continuation, a small set of distinct directions, or the likely underlying problem—then recommend the next concrete move.

When editing a file, preserve its format and edit the file directly. When editing text in chat, avoid surrounding the result with unnecessary preamble or a long account of the editing process.

## Final check

Before returning prose, ask:

- Does the reader know where they are, why this matters, and what comes next?
- Does each paragraph make one useful move?
- Does new information arrive after the context needed to understand it?
- Are complicated ideas grounded in examples?
- Can any sentence be misread on the first pass?
- Are specificity, concision, rhythm, and repetition serving the intended voice?
- Does the piece keep its promises and earn its conclusion?
- Did the edit preserve facts, uncertainty, citations, and the writer's identity?
